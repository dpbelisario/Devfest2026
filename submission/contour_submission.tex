\section{Project Overview}

``SEAR'' (Student Evacuation AR) is a phone app that gets you where you need to go, fast, in your own language.
In an emergency it finds the nearest official evacuation site, guides you to the nearest
exit of the building, and points the way with 3D AR arrows on the camera view. On an
ordinary day it is a campus guide that takes students to buildings and classrooms, floor
by floor. A Gemini assistant answers questions about sites, warnings and directions.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Supported languages (expandable):} English, Japanese, Chinese and Korean
  \item \textbf{Platforms:} Android and iOS apps. Both share one interface, and native code
        adds the camera, sensor and AR features. A browser version is also available for quick
        testing, without the native features.
  \item \textbf{Data:} 1,735 official evacuation sites for Nagoya City (GSI open data)
        and Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA) warnings, refreshed automatically
  \item \textbf{Reliability:} Only official designations are shown. Opening status and
        capacity stay ``unknown'' instead of being guessed, and the assistant points
        users to official instructions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Problems Addressed}

\subsection{Target Users}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=4pt]
  \item University students who need to find a building or classroom fast
  \item Anyone who needs to reach the nearest evacuation site fast
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Problems and Rationale}

\noindent\textbf{Problem:} Whether an earthquake hits mid-lecture or a new student is racing to
class, people lose precious minutes because alerts and evacuation data come in Japanese and
nothing shows which exit, which way, or which classroom to head for.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Solution:} SEAR gets you there fast, in your own language.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{To the evacuation site, fast.} One tap shows the nearest official site.
        SEAR guides you to the nearest exit of the building, then 3D AR arrows on the camera
        view point the way. When an official alert arrives on Android, SEAR opens by itself
        with what to do now and the nearest suitable site.
  \item \textbf{To the classroom, fast.} Pick the building, your starting point and your
        destination, floor by floor, and follow the route on the floor map or with 3D AR.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{14pt}
\subsection{Why We Chose Google Technologies}

\begin{answerbox}
We needed a working, multilingual app within a short hackathon. Google Cloud and Firebase
let us put the website, the API, the database, the scheduled data imports and the AI
assistant in one project, without managing servers.

Gemini's function calling was the key piece. Instead of letting the model answer from
memory, the assistant calls our own tools to read the official site list and the latest
JMA warnings, so its answers stay tied to real data.
\end{answerbox}

\subsection{Test Procedure}
Open the URL above, ideally on a phone. Choose a language (English, Japanese, Chinese or
Korean) with the globe button in the header, or under \textbf{Menu $\rightarrow$ Settings}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Student version}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=4pt]
  \item Tap \textbf{Student} at the top. Nagoya University buildings appear as blue pins
        with cards at the bottom.
  \item On the \textbf{Central Library} card, tap \textbf{Details}, then
        \textbf{Indoor navigation}.
  \item Choose the \textbf{floor}, your \textbf{starting checkpoint} and a
        \textbf{destination} (the 2F exit or the nearest stairs on that floor), then tap
        \textbf{I am at this checkpoint $\cdot$ start}. The route appears on the official
        floor map.
  \item Back on the card, tap \textbf{3D AR directions}. In the Android app the camera opens
        and asks \textbf{Where are you now?} Choose your starting point and tap \textbf{Start}.
        3D AR arrows then guide you to the library exit.
  \item Drag the grey bar on top of a card down to shrink the cards to one row (name and
        \textbf{3D AR}), and drag it up to bring the full cards back.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Evacuation version}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=4pt]
  \item Tap \textbf{Evacuation} at the top. Official Nagoya evacuation sites appear as green
        pins, nearest first.
  \item On the nearest site's card, tap \textbf{3D AR directions}. When asked
        ``Are you inside a building right now?'', tap \textbf{Yes, I'm inside}.
  \item Choose your \textbf{starting room and floor}. The destination is the
        \textbf{nearest exit}. Tap \textbf{Start} and follow the indoor directions to the exit
        (stairs only, no elevators, sample floor plan).
  \item After the exit, SEAR continues with \textbf{3D AR} arrows on the camera view to
        the evacuation site.
  \item (Android app only) Open the menu and tap \textbf{Test alert}. After a few seconds
        the emergency pop-up should open with what to do now and the nearest site.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Where, How and Why We Used AI}

We used AI in two places: to build the app, and inside the app itself.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt,topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Development:} We wrote much of the code with AI coding agents. The
        repository's \texttt{AGENTS.md} sets the rules they follow: keep the code readable
        for a small team, never present demo data as real evacuation guidance, run the type
        checks, lint, tests and build, and report each change on our shared GitHub issue.
        This let a small team build the Android app, the iOS app, their shared interface
        and the backend within the hackathon.
  \item \textbf{SEAR AI (Gemini on Vertex AI):} The assistant uses function calling.
        It looks up the official site list and the latest JMA warnings with our own tools,
        then replies in the user's language and can show sites on the map or start AR.
        Grounding the answers in our data keeps them from inventing sites or warnings.
  \item \textbf{Photo landmarks (Gemini image understanding):} Inside the Central Library,
        a user can take a photo of a nearby sign. Gemini reads the sign text and suggests
        matching landmarks from our catalog. The user confirms the landmark before the
        position changes, because a model's guess should not move someone's location.
  \item \textbf{On-device AI:} On Android, ML Kit translates text offline and Android
        speech recognition turns spoken questions into text. On iOS, Apple Vision text
        recognition reads room labels on imported floor plans without uploading the image.
\end{itemize}

AI-generated code and answers were reviewed by development team members through functional
testing and checks against the official data before being adopted. Code changes were merged
through pull requests after automated checks (TypeScript type checks, lint, backend unit tests
and browser UI tests). In the app, Gemini answers only from our tool results, requests are
rate-limited, and replies point users to official instructions instead of guessing.

\section{What We Gained and How Much We Grew Through Development}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt,topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{From open data to a usable service:} We learned to turn official files into
        something people can act on. Evacuation sites are imported every hour and JMA
        warnings every minute with Cloud Scheduler and Workflows, and checked before saving.
  \item \textbf{Keeping AI grounded:} Function calling taught us to let Gemini work from our
        data instead of its memory. Caching and rate limits taught us to watch cost from the start.
  \item \textbf{Limits:} Sensors alone cannot guide someone indoors reliably. Phone compasses
        drift and step counting adds up small errors, so good indoor navigation still needs
        human-informed data, such as accurate floor plans, checked routes and confirmed
        landmarks. Until that data exists for a building, we label routes as estimates.
  \item \textbf{Working as a team across platforms:} Sharing one interface and shared Kotlin
        code let Android and iOS move together. Pull requests and a shared GitHub issue kept
        four people working on the same app in a short time.
